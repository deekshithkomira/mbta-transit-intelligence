# Solutions playbook

_Generated by `mbta-ti analyze`; numbers are pulled from the analysis outputs._

Every problem below is measured in this project's data. Every countermeasure has a measured result in Boston or a peer system, cited at the bottom of each card. The playbook joins them into one system: measure → make paying easy → harden → enforce smartly → reinforce the norm, with fixes for accessibility, reliability and ridership alongside.

## 1. Measure evasion continuously, in the open
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · measurement

The MBTA publishes a 'fare engagement' ratio but no evasion rate; the newest official estimate (buses) uses 2019 data. You cannot manage what you do not measure, and riders' beliefs about how common evasion is drive the behaviour.

- Run the MassDOT method (fare taps matched to automatic passenger counts) as a monthly job on every bus and Green Line trip, not a one-off study.
- Publish a quarterly evasion rate by mode with confidence intervals, as TfL (3.5%, 2025/26) and the New York State Comptroller already do.
- Add BART's 'did you witness fare evasion?' question to the Rider Census; it is how BART tracked its drop from 25% to 10%.

_Evidence:_ MassDOT bus compliance study (22% non-interaction, 2019 data); TfL; NY OSC; BART (May 2026); Celse & Grolleau (Transport Policy, 2023).

## 2. Close the reduced-fare gap before policing it
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · affordability

65% of riders meet the MBTA's low-income definition, but only 9.6% ride on any reduced fare and 74% pay full fare out of pocket. Part of evasion is an affordability problem that enforcement cannot fix.

- Auto-enrol eligible riders through data-sharing with DTA/MassHealth instead of an opt-in application (the income-eligible program has ~38,000 riders).
- Apply fare capping to contactless and CharlieCard taps so that heavy low-income riders never pay more than a monthly pass.
- Target outreach by line: the survey shows the gap is widest on buses.

_Evidence:_ MBTA 2025 Rider Census (n=145,468); MBTA Income-Eligible Reduced Fare reports; Boston Route 28 fare-free pilot (MBTA: +22% fare-attributable ridership).

## 3. Harden the gates that carry the most riders first
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · gate jumping & tailgating

Taller, full-height gate panels are the best-evidenced single fix: WMATA reported an 82% drop in rail evasion after retrofitting all 98 stations, and New York's 20-station pilot cut evasion 35% on average. The MBTA has no subway gate-hardening program.

- Phase 1 retrofit at the stations this model ranks highest for exposure: South Station, North Station, Downtown Crossing, Back Bay, Harvard.
- Use wide-aisle double-door gates for accessibility and strollers, so hardening does not push riders to the emergency gate.
- Alarm and camera-cover emergency gates; dwell-time sensors catch tailgating (two bodies, one tap) without facial recognition.
- Extend commuter-rail gates (South Station activations +33%, Ruggles +44%).

_Evidence:_ WMATA (Sept 2024); NY Governor's office (Oct 2026); MBTA/Keolis via MassLive (Sept 2026). Vendor camera-detection claims are not yet independently evaluated, so pilot with a control group.

## 4. Randomised, optimised inspection instead of visible crackdowns
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · enforcement

At a $50 fine and $2.40 fare, deterrence needs ~4.8% of trips checked — ~15,379 checks a weekday at gated stations. The MBTA reported 88 warnings and 0 citations in 2025. Capacity is the constraint, so where and when inspectors stand matters most.

- Schedule with this project's optimiser: at the same staffing it deters 257% more evasion than an even spread and 54% more than busiest-station-first, and it never does worse in any of the 30 sensitivity cases.
- Generate rosters by randomised sampling from the optimal coverage, so evaders cannot learn the pattern (random controls beat concentrated crackdowns).
- Issue citations, not only warnings, after a published grace period; without a credible penalty the expected cost of evading is close to zero.
- Prioritise Green Line surface stops (65 of 78 Green/Mattapan stations are ungated) with proof-of-payment checks on board.

_Evidence:_ Barabino et al. (Transp. Res. B, 2014): optimal POP inspection ≈3.4–4.0%; Dai, Galeotti & Villeval (2023); MA Inspector General letter (Sept 2025).

## 5. Tell riders the truth: most people pay
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · social norms

In a field experiment at French rail stations, posters with an eye cue alone did nothing. Paired with a message about how most people pay, they reduced detected evasion.

- Put descriptive-norm messages at gate arrays and rear doors, citing the published evasion rate.
- A/B-test messages by station and score them with the continuous measurement above.

_Evidence:_ Ayal, Celse & Hochman (OBHDP 166, 2021).

## 6. Short-Hop: pay by distance — end to end of a line costs $2.40
**Problem area:** Fare evasion · fare design

Copley → Arlington costs the same $2.40 as Copley → Medford/Tufts (6.0 track miles), so the flat fare is worst value exactly where skipping payment is most tempting. Under Short-Hop, riding any line end to end costs $2.40 and shorter trips pay in proportion: Copley → Medford/Tufts $1.80, Copley → Arlington the $0.80 minimum. No fare ever rises.

- Tap in and tap out with the Short-Hop app or a contactless card; the fare is set on exit from composite miles (average of track and straight-line distance, as Washington Metrorail measures it), rounded up to $0.05.
- A missing tap-out is charged $2.40 (MBTA gates validate entry only, so skipping the exit tap must never be cheaper). Students and seniors pay half.
- Pilot and test break-even: a $0.80 short trip needs 200% more paying riders on it; Copley → Medford/Tufts at $1.80 needs 33%.

_Evidence:_ WMATA Tariff 49 (effective July 1, 2026) for composite miles, rounding and the min:max ratio; MBTA $2.40 flat fare; MBTA Rapid Transit Stop Distances.

## 7. Treat elevators as critical service, not facilities
**Problem area:** Accessibility

In 2025 the MBTA posted 2,062 elevator outage alerts. Chinatown (elevator) alone was out 2,881 hours. No official elevator availability rate is published.

- Publish elevator availability % per station monthly, with an SLA for repair time.
- Predictive maintenance: rank units by outage recurrence (this project's alert history) and pre-stage parts at chronic stations.
- Push outage alerts to trip planners with an automatic accessible-route reroute.

_Evidence:_ MBTA Service Alerts 2025 (open data), parsed per asset.

## 8. Fix the worst routes where the most riders feel it
**Problem area:** Reliability

Route 35 ran 47.1% on time over the last 12 months; Franklin Line was the least reliable commuter rail line at 85.9%. Subway arrival predictions were 79.7% accurate in 2026.

- Bus-priority lanes and signal priority on the bottom-decile routes from the empirical-Bayes ranking (ranked on credibility, not noisy raw rates).
- Lock in the slow-zone gains: alert when any line's restricted share rises above zero for 30 days — median slow zone lifetimes were Blue 30d, Green 77d, Orange 61d, Red 53d.
- Improve subway prediction accuracy; trust in countdown clocks shapes whether riders choose the T over rideshare.

_Evidence:_ MBTA Bus/CR Reliability, Speed Restrictions, Prediction Accuracy (open data). Rider Census: rideshare is now riders' top alternative (43%).

## 9. Win back the Red Line rider
**Problem area:** Ridership & revenue

The Red Line is at 61.9% of 2019 weekday ridership, the lowest of any line, even though its slow zones are gone. Every lost rider is lost fare revenue that no enforcement program can recover.

- Market the new speed: measured excess delay fell ~75% from 2023 to 2025.
- Off-peak frequency and fare products aimed at hybrid workers (flexible 10-ride / capped weekly fares).
- Track recovery by station monthly using the gate-tap pipeline in this project.

_Evidence:_ MBTA Monthly Ridership, Gated Station Entries, Heavy Rail Excess Trip Time.
